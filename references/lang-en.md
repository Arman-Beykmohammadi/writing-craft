# Language: English

Load this file for any English text or English section. It holds the English settings and English writing rules. English markers live in the catalog entries (`patterns.md`, lines marked EN). Rule IDs start with `EN-`.

The English vocabulary lists and several thresholds in the catalog come from avoid-ai-writing, which calibrated its detector on English text only. Its maintainers note that the "appears far more often in AI text" claim behind its Tier 1A list is inherited from another project and not measured by them. Treat the lists as well-supported conventions, not statistics.

## EN-1 Spelling variety

- Keep the document's variety: American (analyze, color, résumé or resume) or British and Commonwealth (analyse, colour, CV). Oxford spelling (-ize with British forms) is also correct. Do not switch without being asked.
- "Resume" (US) and "CV" (UK, Europe, academia) name different documents in the US (see genre-cv.md, CV-10).
- Keep one variety across a document and its companion documents (CV and cover letter).

## EN-2 Punctuation

- Dashes: see R6. Em dashes (—) are legitimate English punctuation, and many careful writers use them; the problem is the dash used as a universal connector. Date and number ranges use an en dash without spaces (2019–2022, pp. 10–12).
- Serial (Oxford) comma: follow the document or the house style.
- Quotation marks: curly or straight, following the document (M5). American style puts periods and commas inside closing quotation marks; British style often puts them outside unless they belong to the quote. Keep the document's convention.
- Semicolons are fine in moderation; do not replace them all.
- Exclamation marks: none in CVs and papers; rare in letters.

## EN-3 Numbers and dates

- Thousands separator comma (12,500); decimal point (3.5).
- Percent without a space (5%) in most house styles.
- Spell out one to nine in running prose in many styles (Chicago spells out to one hundred); use numerals in CVs, tables, and technical text. Follow the document.
- Dates: "25 September 2026" (UK) or "September 25, 2026" (US). In international documents avoid "03/04/2026", which reads as March in the US and April elsewhere. CVs: "Mar 2021 – Jun 2023" or "03/2021 – 06/2023", one format throughout.

## EN-4 Contractions and register

- CVs: no contractions.
- Papers, theses, proposals: no contractions in most fields.
- Cover letters and formal emails: occasional contractions are fine in many industries; follow the user's draft.
- Blog posts, LinkedIn, casual messages: contractions are normal; removing them makes text stiff.

## EN-5 Headings

- Sentence case by default ("Results and discussion"), unless the house style or venue requires title case. CV section headings may use either.
- No emoji, no trailing colons, no numbering unless the document numbers sections.

## EN-6 Plain words

The catalog's Tier 1B clarity list (I4) covers "utilize", "in order to", "due to the fact that", "commence", "ascertain", "endeavor", and the like. These are wordiness, not evidence of AI writing. Replace them when the user wants tighter prose; never present them as authorship signals.

## EN-7 Writers working in a second language

Many English texts this skill sees are written by people for whom English is a second language, especially CVs, papers, and applications. Several catalog patterns fire on second-language writing: uniform sentence structure, formal vocabulary, careful grammar, transitions such as "Moreover" learned from textbooks. Rules:

- Never call such text AI-written.
- Frame findings as clarity and register advice.
- Keep correct but unusual idioms if the meaning is clear; the goal is not to erase the writer's origin.
- Fix real errors (articles, prepositions, tense) only when the user asks for proofreading or when an error changes the meaning; list them separately from style findings.
